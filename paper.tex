\begin{abstract}
We investigate cooperation between learned decisions and constraint optimisation for protein folding in the three-dimensional hydrophobic--polar lattice model. DecisionBoost combines a transformer fold constructor, a learned repair-region controller and bounded CP-SAT search; verified solver improvements supply feedback for learning. A completed local pilot motivates further evaluation but does not establish superiority over reinforcement learning or specialised HP solvers. This revised working draft specifies two distinct experiments: a CPU comparison of CP-SAT, an attention-based DQN and a fixed RL--CP-SAT hybrid on eight benchmark sequences, and a fresh-sequence replication of the learned DecisionBoost repair policy. The CPU campaign uses five seeds, a common declared lattice cube and a configurable elapsed budget, provisionally 24 hours per run. Jobs request Apocrita's ten-day scheduler ceiling while application guards stop computation earlier and enforce an absolute ten-day campaign deadline. Historical Apple MPS results, published reference values and new CPU measurements are kept separate. Cluster results remain pending; the paper's conclusions will follow the completed, independently checked trajectories rather than the available allocation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Dill introduced the Hydrophobic-Polar (HP) lattice model in 1985~\cite{Dill1985}. This model collapses the twenty amino acid types into two classes: hydrophobic residues (H), which tend to avoid water, and polar residues (P), which interact more favourably with it. A sequence is then embedded as a self-avoiding walk on a lattice, with each residue occupying a distinct site and successive residues constrained to lie on adjacent lattice points~\cite{Lau1989}. Within this discrete setting, the objective is driven by hydrophobic contacts. In the simpler version of the model, the energy is defined by the number of non-consecutive H-H contacts, i.e., pairs of hydrophobic residues that are neighbours on the lattice but not adjacent along the chain. In effect, the optimisation target is to find an embedding that promotes hydrophobic clustering while respecting the self-avoidance and adjacency constraints.

Even under this simplified lattice formulation, HP folding remains computationally demanding. \textit{Crescenzi et al.}\ proved in 1998 that the optimisation problem is NP-complete~\cite{Crescenzi1998}, so one should not expect a method that scales efficiently across all instances as the sequence length increases. The practical consequences show up fairly quickly: by the time sequences are in the 30 to 50 residue range, the set of valid self-avoiding conformations is already large enough that exhaustive enumeration stops being a useful point of comparison. That is part of the reason the HP model is used so often in combinatorial optimisation research: it is clean to specify, hard to solve, and easy to evaluate consistently across algorithms.

Given that hardness, much of the earlier computational literature relied on heuristics rather than exact optimisation. Simulated annealing became a common choice from the 1990s onward because it provides a straightforward mechanism for escaping local minima by occasionally accepting unfavourable moves early in the search~\cite{UngerMoult1993}. Genetic algorithms offered a different style of exploration, maintaining a population of candidate folds and gradually improving it through recombination and mutation operators~\cite{Krasnogor2002Multimeme}. Tabu search was also explored, discouraging cycling by remembering recent states. Ant colony optimisation took yet another approach, biasing construction using information carried over from successful searches~\cite{LeshPullMoves2003, StützleACO2005}.

Constraint programming (CP) offers an alternative to heuristic search for HP folding. Chain connectivity, self-avoidance, and contact formation can all be encoded directly as folding constraints and then pruned through propagation~\cite{Barták2003}. Earlier CP tools, such as CPSP-tools, successfully tackled HP folding using specialised H-core databases and constraint satisfaction techniques~\cite{CPSP2009}. Google OR-Tools CP-SAT extends this approach by combining it with on-the-fly SAT-style clause learning and linear programming relaxation~\cite{Perron2023}. This can provide bounds on solution quality and, in favourable cases, certificates of optimality~\cite{GoogleORTools}. Large Neighbourhood Search is also supported, allowing solutions to be improved iteratively~\cite{Shaw1998}. Earlier domain-specific tools such as CPSP-tools~\cite{CPSP2009} solve HP folding by enumerating a precomputed database of optimal H-cores (connected lattice subgraphs of hydrophobic residues), then asking a constraint solver only whether the remaining polar residues can be threaded around each candidate core; the applicability of that offline precomputation depends on the lattice and model variant. Our CP-SAT formulation instead encodes the full folding problem directly, trading some HP-specific power for portability, reproducibility, and direct comparability against a learning-based baseline on the same benchmark family.


Attention-based deep Q-learning treats folding as a sequential decision problem~\cite{AttnDRL2504}. Its training cost and solver search cost must be measured explicitly when comparing methods. A common CPU experiment answers a question about performance under a specified CPU allocation; it does not establish how GPU-accelerated RL would perform. An exact solver certifies optimality only when its status and bound establish that claim for the domain actually searched.

Our central question is whether a learned model can identify folding decisions and repair regions for which bounded constraint search produces useful improvements. We distinguish this DecisionBoost hypothesis from the simpler fixed RL--CP-SAT hybrid. The contribution being tested is the learned HP-specific constructor/controller/solver cooperation, together with checked witnesses and reproducible cost accounting; we do not claim that hybrid optimisation itself is new.

\section{Problem and Methods}
\subsection{HP Folding and Independent Verification}
The HP protein folding problem on a three-dimensional lattice is defined as follows. A protein sequence is represented as a string:
\begin{equation}
S = s_1 s_2 \ldots s_n,
\end{equation}
where each residue $s_i$ is classified as either hydrophobic (H) or polar (P). A conformation $C$ is a self-avoiding walk on the three-dimensional integer lattice $\mathbb{Z}^3$, meaning each residue is placed at a distinct position:
\begin{equation}
c_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i) \in \mathbb{Z}^3,
\end{equation}
and no two residues occupy the same lattice point. The adjacency constraint requires that consecutive residues along the chain are placed at Manhattan distance 1:
\begin{equation}
||c_i - c_{i+1}||_1 = 1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \ldots, n-1\}.
\end{equation}
where $||v||_1 = |x| + |y| + |z|$ for a vector $v = (x, y, z)$.

The energy of a conformation is determined by the number of non-consecutive H-H contacts. A contact occurs when two hydrophobic residues are neighbours on the lattice but not adjacent along the sequence. Formally, the energy function is defined as
\begin{equation}
E(C) = -\left|\{(i,j) : i<j, \, s_i = s_j = \text{H}, \, j-i > 1, \, ||c_i - c_j||_1 = 1\}\right|,
\end{equation}
where the set counts all pairs of non-consecutive H-residues that are lattice-adjacent. The objective is to minimise $E(C)$, which is equivalent to maximising the number of favourable hydrophobic contacts. This formulation reflects the simplified physical principle that hydrophobic residues prefer to cluster together, shielding themselves from the surrounding aqueous environment. An illustration of this energy function is provided in Figure~\ref{fig:hp_lattice}.

Every reported solution is a complete fold. An independent integer-coordinate checker verifies anchoring, unit backbone bonds, self-avoidance and the contact count without importing the neural model or solver. Individual contact counts are integers; fractional reported scores are averages over sequences or seeds. An absent solution remains missing rather than becoming a zero-contact solution.

\subsection{Compact CP-SAT Model}\label{sec:cpsat_approach}
The primary CPU implementation uses one integer coordinate triple per residue and an injective integer site identifier. Let $R=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. The common domain is $|x_i|,|y_i|,|z_i|\le R$, with the origin and first bond fixed. Reachability additionally bounds each coordinate of zero-based residue $i$ by $i$. An \texttt{AllDifferent} constraint on the site identifiers enforces self-avoidance. Absolute coordinate differences impose unit backbone distance. For each non-consecutive H--H pair of opposite chain parity, a Boolean is true if and only if its Manhattan distance equals one. Maximising their sum therefore maximises the independently recomputed contact count~\cite{GoogleORTools,Perron2023}.

A fresh CP-only run searches the full declared cube without a heuristic hint. A repair fixes all coordinates outside its movable window, retains both boundary bonds and every self-avoidance constraint, and constrains the contact objective not to fall below the incumbent. The incumbent is retained on solver failure. \texttt{OPTIMAL} in a repair refers only to that neighbourhood; \texttt{OPTIMAL} in a full primary search refers only to the declared cube. Neither is automatically an unrestricted lattice proof. Restarted solves do not retain a previous solve's learned clauses.

\subsection{Project Attention-DQN Baseline}\label{sec:lstma_approach}
The bundled project reference uses an explicit $(n,5)$ observation: coordinates, residue type and normalised chain index, with sentinels for unplaced residues. A duelling transformer estimates five relative placement actions. Training retains the reference Double-DQN target, prioritised replay, Adam optimiser, FP32 precision, valid-action mask and at most one replay update per episode. The mask also retains its reference symmetry restrictions and one-step trap lookahead. Separate models are initialised for each sequence and seed. This source is associated with the attention-based RL comparison~\cite{AttnDRL2504}, but the frozen project configurations in Table~\ref{tab:cluster_configs} are not silently substituted for the published protocol.

The exploration schedule is fixed over 200,000 training episodes even when an elapsed-time cutoff truncates training. Periodic greedy evaluation restores the previous training mode in a \texttt{finally} block. If 200,000 episodes finish before the elapsed budget, training stops and the frozen policy continues sampling with exploration probability 0.05; those samples and their costs are identified separately. The old MPS execution backend is not used in the CPU campaign.

\subsection{Fixed RL--CP-SAT Hybrid}
The fixed hybrid uses the same DQN training configuration and adds a bounded CP repair every 1,000 episodes once a valid complete incumbent exists. Ten movable residues alternate between a suffix and a central internal window, with a 30-second maximum repair allowance. Model construction, solving and verification are charged to the same elapsed budget as RL training. CP-generated folds update the search incumbent but do not modify the RL replay buffer. This arm tests simple cooperation; it is not the learned DecisionBoost policy.

\subsection{DecisionBoost: Constructor, Controller and Solver}
A separate experiment replicates the tested DecisionBoost architecture. A frozen, pinned ModernBERT-large encoder supplies full-sequence residue features. A fresh two-layer, width-64 transformer constructor receives those features together with explicit geometry and legal candidate features. A separate small transformer controller selects from suffix and internal repair windows of sizes 6, 10 and 16. CP-SAT produces verified improvements; elite improved folds provide constructor feedback, while observed repair gains supply controller targets. Tied maximal witnessed gains receive equal target mass. These targets describe bounded search outcomes, not exact action values or proofs that an unproductive action is infeasible.

Each seed has fresh constructor/controller/optimiser states. The prescribed training schedule consists of 1,200 initial constructor steps, 256 solver-feedback steps and 1,200 controller steps. Evaluation freezes both learned models and compares learned repair selection with fixed, random, training-selected static and cheap-proposal controls. The pretrained encoder is reused; its original pretraining cost is unknown and is not described as zero. This is an open transformer decision prototype, not a Jev API call or an RL-trained folding policy.

\section{Predeclared Cluster Experiments}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Hardware and Reproducibility}
Primary runs target a verified homogeneous Apocrita EHC CPU selection. Current documentation describes AMD EPYC 9965 nodes, with jobs limited to 96 cores each~\cite{ApocritaEHC}. The provisional allocation is eight cores and 32 GB per run; a disposable pilot screens CPU thread counts 1, 2, 4 and 8 on 3d4, 3d6 and 3d8 before the common allocation is frozen. Primary runs record host, CPU model, Python/library versions, allocated cores, seeds, exact HP strings and executed-source hashes. We do not infer EPYC throughput from Apple laptop measurements.

\subsection{Scheduler Ceiling, Compute Cutoff and Campaign Deadline}
Apocrita recommends one-hour requests for its short partitions or a 240-hour ceiling for standard partitions~\cite{ApocritaRuntime}. The supplied campaign scripts request ten days but apply a separate, configurable compute cutoff. The provisional scientific budget is 24 hours per primary sequence/seed/method run, not ten days of mandatory computation. The budget is selected after the pilot and remains common across the three primary methods.

The complete campaign expires ten days after preparation/submission, including queue time. The worker stops launching work before that deadline; a separate parent process signals it at its per-run cutoff and forcibly terminates its own process group after a bounded saving grace. Checkpoints preserve networks, optimiser, replay priorities, update counts and random-number states. Explicit resume keeps the original deadline and charges previously consumed time; no automatic requeue or extension occurs. Late-starting and interrupted runs remain partial.

\subsection{Primary CPU Comparison}
The eight frozen benchmark strings have lengths 20, 24, 25, 36, 46, 48, 50 and 58. Five seeds per method give 120 runs. The shared optimisation endpoint is the best independently validated complete contact count obtained by each elapsed-time threshold, with trajectories at one, two, twelve and twenty-four hours and additional thresholds when the chosen budget permits. Greedy RL evaluation and training completion are reported separately from the best search incumbent. Full declared-cube proofs can justify an early solver finish; other premature finishes do not acquire fictitious budget coverage.

Final tables require coverage at the requested budget or an applicable exact-domain proof. We report missing-solution frequency, completed-run counts, paired per-seed differences, descriptive variability and uncertainty appropriate to five seeds. Queue time, allocated CPU-hours and checkpoint/output overhead are reported alongside elapsed compute time. A short-time outcome does not establish eventual learning convergence. Historical GPU results and published best-seed values provide context only and are not pooled into CPU means.

\subsection{Separate Fresh-Sequence DecisionBoost Replication}
The optional five-pipeline experiment uses a new frozen corpus excluding reversal-equivalent sequences from previous local pilots and the benchmark set. It contains 128 training and 32 reserved validation sequences at lengths 16, 20, 28 and 36, and 72 test sequences, twelve each at lengths 16, 20, 28, 36, 48 and 58. Validation instances do not tune this fixed replication protocol. Every pipeline evaluates the same held-out strings.

This experiment preserves the original complete reachable coordinate domains, origin/+x anchoring and single-worker CP search caps. Each fresh test case uses sixteen constructor proposals and four one-second repairs per policy; cold CP uses one four-second search. Equal solver call caps do not imply equal elapsed time. Encoder loading/encoding, cheap archive generation, solver teaching, neural training, proposal inference and online repair costs are logged separately. These results answer the learned repair-policy question and must not be merged into the primary restricted-cube elapsed-budget table. The protocol finishes when its prescribed cases finish, even if the cluster allocation has time remaining.

\section{Existing Pilot Evidence and Pending Results}
The completed laptop engineering screen found no candidate optimisation meeting its predeclared ten-percent throughput threshold. MPS was approximately 4.2--5.4 times faster than the best tested CPU setting for its three representative disposable workloads. This identifies a local bottleneck; it does not predict the cluster runtime or establish learning performance.

A separate completed local DecisionBoost pilot used five fresh pipelines and 72 held-out sequences per pipeline. Learned repair selection improved mean final contacts by approximately 0.61 over a training-selected static repair policy and 1.11 over fixed ten-residue suffix repair under the tested call caps. The reported sequence-group bootstrap interval for the former difference was approximately 0.38--0.87 contacts, conditional on that finite test set. The experiment lasted about 2 hours 55 minutes overall. Its independent audit checked saved solver witnesses and protocol traces. It included no matched RL arm and did not establish end-to-end equal-time superiority. This evidence motivates the fresh cluster replication; it is not a cluster result.

The cluster result tables and quality-versus-time figures will be populated only after execution and independent verification. No optimality, RL superiority, CPU speedup or convergence conclusion is inferred from the planned allocation.

\section{Discussion and Claim Boundaries}\label{sec:discussion}
The CPU experiment and the DecisionBoost replication address different questions. The former measures achievable quality under a common CPU allocation and elapsed budget. The latter isolates learned versus fixed repair selection using matched solver allowances and separately accounted offline costs. A CPU result does not describe GPU-accelerated RL, and a call-budget result does not automatically establish an elapsed-time advantage.

Combining neural proposals with a solver does not itself prove novelty or improved performance. The HP-specific learned decisions, verified solver feedback and ablation results must support the eventual contribution. Specialised CPSP/H-core methods remain relevant calibration references~\cite{CPSP2009}; comparison against general-purpose CP-SAT alone cannot establish superiority over exact HP methods. Bounds and optimality claims must name their domain, and the HP lattice task remains a combinatorial benchmark rather than a realistic all-atom folding model.
